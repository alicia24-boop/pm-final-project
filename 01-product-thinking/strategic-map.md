# Strategic Discovery Map (Module 1)

## Responses
- **Write one feature from the map that, if an engineer asked “why is this a priority for our 12-week roadmap?”, you honestly could not give a strategic answer for.:** Social media sharing.. It sounds like a reasonable feature for a habit-tracking app, but I don’t have evidence that users actually want it, that it solves an important hydration problem, or that it would meaningfully support our business goals. So I couldn’t justify prioritizing it in a 12-week roadmap.
- **Write one feature that looks “correct” for the app but has zero strategic weight, you have no idea if it helps the company make money or whether a single user actually asked for it.:** Streaks & achievements. It feels like a natural feature for a habit-tracking app, but we don’t know if users actually want gamification, whether it improves retention, or whether it contributes to revenue. Without that evidence, it looks reasonable but has very little strategic weight.
- **Compare V3 to your Step 1 baseline. What changed, and is the output objectively better, did the AI get smarter, or did your added PM knowledge (JTBD, effort/impact, retention) force it to be useful?:** V3 is much more useful because it connects ideas to specific user jobs, friction points, effort vs. impact, monetization, and retention instead of just listing possible features. The AI didn’t necessarily get smarter—the added PM context and frameworks gave it better constraints and forced the output to be more strategic and actionable.
- **Key takeaway: how does this prove the PM’s role is to think while the AI’s role is to execute?:** The PM has to define the problem, user needs, priorities, constraints, and tradeoffs. AI can then execute quickly by generating and organizing ideas. Without the PM’s judgment and strategic context, AI may produce plausible features, but it can’t determine which ones are actually worth building.
